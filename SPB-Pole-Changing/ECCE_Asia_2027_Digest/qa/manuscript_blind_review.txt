Abstract—Stacked polyphase bridges (SPBs) enable low-voltage power devices to supply high-voltage traction systems while independently controlling multiple stator winding groups. This paper presents a device–winding co-design method that exploits these groups for electronic pole changing. Discrete GaN voltage ratings, cell-voltage stress, coil allocation, three-phase balance, and pole ratio are treated as coupled constraints. A constructive winding analysis compares 2:1, 3:1, and 5:2 pole ratios. For a 1.2-kV link, the investigated design space includes a 150-V, 16-cell, 48-slot 8/4-pole configuration and a 200-V, 12-cell, 36-slot 12/6-pole configuration. The 2:1 ratio provides a minimum pitch factor of 0.866 with moderate coil span; 5:2 permits higher pitch utilization at substantially longer span. Megawatt-level charging of heavy-duty electric vehicles (EVs) motivates higher battery and dc-link voltages to limit current and cable loss at a given power [1]. In a conventional two-level traction inverter, each switch must block the full dc-link voltage together with switching overshoot. Increasing the blocking-voltage requirement introduces conduction- and switching-loss trade-offs that constrain the simultaneous improvement of efficiency and switching frequency. Multilevel inverters provide an alternative to full-link-voltage switching. Three-, five-, and seven-level neutral-point-clamped (NPC) or flying-capacitor (FC) implementations can employ lower-blocking-voltage devices and generate smaller output-voltage steps. With appropriate modulation, these steps can reduce current distortion and CMV [2]. However, the additional switches, clamping or flying-capacitor networks, and balancing functions introduce further failure mechanisms and implementation complexity. Low cell voltages permit suitable low-voltage GaN devices, while the machine is implemented with multiple three-phase winding sets whose neutral points remain isolated. Pole changing can exploit this flexibility to trade low-speed torque against high-speed capability and operating-point losses. Variable-pole induction drives have demonstrated partial-load and high-speed loss benefits [3], and pole-changing EV drives have been investigated for wide-speed traction requirements [4]. Such benefits motivate drive-cycle-dependent pole selection, although net cycle savings must include converter and excitation losses. Conventional implementations use Dahlander-type stator reconnection or multiple inverter units to reverse selected coil-group currents.
