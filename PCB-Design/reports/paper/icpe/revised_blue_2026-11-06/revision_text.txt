Low-Voltage GaN Utilization for High-Voltage Stacked Polyphase Bridge Traction Drives
Topic 17 — Power Electronics for Transportation Electrification
Abstract—The stacked polyphase bridge (SPB) partitions a high-voltage traction dc link among series-connected submodules, enabling the use of low-voltage gallium nitride (GaN) devices. Retaining their loss advantages at traction current requires sufficient drain- and gate-voltage margins in the physical building block. This paper evaluates a phase leg for a 300-kW, 1200-V drive, starting with a charge-based device comparison and a preliminary four-device parallel arrangement using the 100-V EPC2361. A KiCad–Ansys Q3D–LTspice workflow connects the routed prototype to a coupled parasitic network. Despite an estimated local commutation-loop inductance of 0.41 nH, the simulated voltage margin depends on gate-loop inductance, drive settings, and operating current. With a 10-nH gate loop and revised drive settings, sampled positive-current points from 1 A to 322 A remain below the 90-V drain–source target at a 75-V local bus; a 5% bus increase exceeds that target at rated current. The contribution is a quantified assessment of how implementation constraints feed back into SPB voltage partitioning. Experimental validation of one building block is planned for the full paper.
Index Terms—Electrification of transportation, Gallium nitride, stacked polyphase bridge, traction inverter.
I. INTRODUCTION
High dc-link voltages support traction power levels of several hundred kilowatts while requiring substantial phase current [1]. In a conventional two-level bridge, the switches block the full dc-link voltage. The stacked polyphase bridge (SPB) instead connects three-phase bridges in series on the dc side, each supplying an isolated winding set of a common machine [2], [3]. With suitable bypass hardware and winding control, its modular structure can support operation after a submodule fault at reduced capability. This benefit is conditional on the remaining submodules’ voltage and current margins; bypass operation is not evaluated here. Fig. 1 shows the topology and the building block considered.

Fig. 1. (a) SPB and isolated winding sets of a common multiphase machine. (b) Three-phase submodule; each switch represents four parallel GaN devices. The diode denotes reverse conduction, not an intrinsic p–n body diode.
Voltage partitioning enables each SPB submodule to use a lower semiconductor voltage class, creating an opportunity to exploit the favorable on-state resistance and switching characteristics of low-voltage gallium nitride (GaN) devices in high-voltage traction drives [4]. Increasing the submodule count reduces the voltage across each bridge but leaves the phase-current requirement unchanged for fixed total power, dc-link voltage, modulation index, and power factor. Several devices may therefore be required in parallel, making current sharing, gate-drive impedance, and commutation paths central to realizing the expected device benefits.
Previous work establishes SPB modulation and losses [3], dc-link stability [5], and comparisons with alternative GaN converters [6]. Studies of paralleled GaN stages identify commutation inductance, source coupling, and unequal gate impedance as important constraints [7]–[9]. The contribution here is to connect these implementation constraints to the SPB voltage choice quantitatively. A peak-current sizing screen is followed by a routed-network current sweep and voltage/temperature sensitivities. The resulting margins show why a favorable device figure of merit and a small local power loop do not alone establish a usable submodule voltage. The current-range and voltage-variation checks close the gap between preliminary sizing and the routed implementation.

C. Selection of Four Parallel Devices
At 10 nH/0.5 nH, four devices give an estimated 18.6-W phase-leg loss and 70 °C junction temperature at 40 °C ambient under the assumed cooling model. Two devices exceed the 125 °C limit (approximately 144 °C), while three exceed the peak-current voltage screen (approximately 99 V). Four is therefore the smallest count passing this preliminary screen, not the minimum-loss count or proof of full-cycle feasibility. The routed-network assessment must resolve the remaining current-range constraint.

B. Paired Gate Drive and Kelvin Returns
Four devices per switch position are arranged as two symmetric pairs. Each 2EDF7275K dual-channel driver [11] drives two high-side devices and two low-side devices, with an individual gate resistor per device. Two drivers serve the phase leg. Dedicated Kelvin returns separate gate and power-current paths; matched gate impedances and symmetric copper paths limit transient inequality. Present simulations assume synchronous driver commands; timing skew and physical realization of the negative gate bias remain validation items.

The extracted power network contains 29 branches and 406 mutual-inductance terms, using dc resistance and partial inductances at 100 MHz. The approximately 0.41-nH local loop comprises 0.347 nH of copper and 0.062 nH of capacitor equivalent series inductance (ESL) under equal branch excitation at the device/capacitor ports. This scalar covers the PCB/capacitor contribution; device-package and gate-path inductances were not separately extracted. Switching simulations retain the coupled network, not only this scalar value. Gate-loop bounds remain 10/20 nH, split 70% in the forward path and 30% in the return.

C. Switching Margins and Feedback to Voltage Partitioning
Table II shows that increasing turn-on resistance reduces rated-current drain stress at an energy cost. Negative off-state bias suppresses the held-off gate disturbance but does not independently recover the drain margin. Setting D reduces the rated-current peak to 88.8 V, with a mean low-side 90%–10% voltage fall time of 5.9 ns and a 38.7-µJ sum of turn-on/off energies. Its simulated positive branch-current peak spread is 1.15%, using (Ipk,max − Ipk,min)/(Ipk,max + Ipk,min). These values quantify the switching-speed/energy tradeoff rather than establish a global optimum.
For setting D, sampled nominal-current points remain between 88.0 and 88.8 V, whereas setting C reaches 91.8 V near 80 A total current (Fig. 8(c)). The low-current high-side transient therefore remains relevant even when the rated-current result is close to target. The 0.8-V reference is the minimum static threshold at 25 °C [10], not an absolute gate-voltage rating or proof against dynamic turn-on. The revised cases reach approximately −2.4 V minimum gate voltage, within the stated −4/+6-V ratings, but driver compatibility and dynamic behavior require measurement.
At rated current, raising the local bus by 5% to 78.75 V increases setting D’s drain peak to 92.2 V at 27 °C, exceeding the design target. Forced 125 °C device-model cases give 84.6 V at 75 V and 88.4 V at 78.75 V; the extracted copper network is held fixed. Thus, the nominal sixteen-submodule result does not demonstrate the proposed voltage-variation allowance. An illustrative seventeen-submodule allocation gives 74.12 V per module at +5% total bus and 88.1/83.8-V rated-current peaks at 27/125 °C. This is an electrical headroom comparison, not validation of a seventeen-winding machine or its full current range. It demonstrates the architectural consequence of unresolved switching margin: either the network/drive must improve or the voltage partition must change.
VI. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION AND FULL-PAPER SCOPE
One building block will be tested at controlled bus voltage, current, and temperature. Drain and Kelvin-referenced gate transients, individual branch currents, and deskewed switching-energy measurements will be compared with predictions. The planned voltage acquisition bandwidth is at least 500 MHz; current-sensor bandwidth, probe loading, and timing uncertainty will be reported. Negative-bias implementation and driver compatibility will be verified before operation. Because of the digest page limit, the detailed experimental procedure and resulting comparisons are reserved for the full paper; no experimental result is claimed here. Regenerative current, inter-driver skew, parasitic tolerances, insulation, and temperature-dependent dynamic losses remain outside the present simulation evidence.
VII. CONCLUSION
The study links low-voltage GaN selection to the switching margins of an SPB phase leg. A four-device candidate and a 0.41-nH local loop do not by themselves establish feasibility. The routed model identifies a 10-nH gate-loop candidate meeting the sampled nominal 90-V target, but its +5% bus case requires further design change or voltage repartitioning. This quantified feedback, rather than device figure of merit alone, governs the proposed low-voltage GaN utilization.
