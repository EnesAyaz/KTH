Abstract—A six-phase, GaN-based inverter using 8 parallel
connected EPC2304 200 V 5 mΩGaN HEMTs per switch is
presented. The design is configured as two identical three-phase
inverters with the option of a shared DC bus to enable recircu-
lation of power. The design is used to power two mechanically
coupled PMSMs in a dynamometer test rig. The use of GaN
transistors enables the design to operate up to frequencies of 100
kHz. Static and dynamic current sharing of the GaN HEMTs is
ensured by careful consideration of symmetry within the gate
loop layout, and is verified using thermal imaging of the GaN
HEMTs. Each three-phase inverter can reach an output power
of 22.8 kW, with estimated HEMT temperatures not exceeding
70°C at 50 kHz switching frequency.
Index Terms—Gallium Nitride, HEMTs, Paralleling, Traction
Inverter, dv/dt, FEA, Wide Bandgap Devices, Thermal Imaging
I. INTRODUCTION
Wide bandgap devices offer favourable electrical and ther-
mal performance compared to Si devices making them an
attractive replacement in EVs [1]. The introduction of SiC-
based inverters in commercial EVs has already led to increased
switching frequencies compared to older Si-based designs,
such as the Tesla Model 3 at 10 kHz in 2017 [2] versus
the Nissan Leaf at 5 kHz in 2011 [3]. GaN offers a further
improvement over SiC due to superior characteristics such
as lower specific on-state resistance, and lower gate and
output capacitance for comparable device ratings [4]. Under
similar conditions, 650 V GaN HEMTs have been shown to
exhibit x3.2 lower switching losses than SiC MOSFETs [5],
enabling higher switching frequencies [6] and reduced passive
component size that are highly desirable in EV applications.
Although GaN HEMTs can offer improvements in efficiency
and power density in EV inverters, there are still implemen-
tation challenges related to thermal and parasitic problems,
such as high dv/dt reducing motor lifetimes [7], that limit
commercial realisation of GaN-based inverters in EVs [8].

based traction inverters with the necessary flexibility to enable
a full range of testing in a motor drive context. Therefore, the

By having the test motor mechanically coupled to a second
motor and inverter in a dynamometer (dyno) rig, power can be
recirculated back into the DC link of the inverter. This means
that the power supply need only provide power for losses in
the system, greatly increasing the possible power rating of
inverter and motor that can be tested in a laboratory setting.
This is in contrast to using a passive dyno, where the power
provided by the motor is dissipated into the environment. Fig.

The rig is composed of two Plettenburg Nova 15 water
cooled motors with their shafts coupled, as well as a 3D
printed housing and coolant temperature sensors for calori-
metric measurements of motor losses.
The current handling capability of individual GaN HEMTs
is constrained by their device structure, limiting achievable
die size and maximum drain current [9]. In high current ap-
plications such as EV traction inverters, paralleling of multiple
GaN HEMTs per switch is necessary.

Parallel connected GaN HEMTs should share current
equally to prevent the unequal heating of one particular
HEMT, which increases probability of failure of said HEMT
[10]. By matching the gate loop impedances, the turn-on and
turn-off of the HEMTs should occur simultaneously, ensuring
equal switching losses [11]. It is also important that the layout
of the half bridge promotes good steady state current sharing
by balancing the current pathways of each HEMT such that
the total resistance of each pathway is equal.

II. DESIGN
A motherboard PCB mounts six half bridge power modules,
organised into two three-phase inverters. The motherboard also
houses the bulk DC link capacitors and current sensors for
each phase and the DC links. The system is controlled by
an STM32F410RB microcontroller. The modules attach to the
motherboard using an array of pin header connectors. Fig.
2 shows the complete GaN-based inverter, including all six
modules, and the populated motherboard PCB.
Fig. 3 is a electrical schematic detailing one of the modules
in the three-phase inverter. Each half bridge power module
is composed of a separate half bridge and gate driver PCB.
This is to help facilitate fault isolation, debugging, and ease
modification of the PCBs.
A. Half Bridge PCB Design
The half bridge PCB is composed of four layers, with the
outside layers being 4 oz copper, and the inside layers being 2
oz copper. The top side of the half bridge PCB is used to house
the main bulk of the components including GaN HEMTs, DC
link capacitors, NTC thermistor, and copper pours for the
Vdc, PGND, and switch node. The bottom side has all the
pin headers for power and signals from the motherboards and
the HEMT gate and source connections for the gate driver
PCB, and a switch node copper pour. The middle layers of
the PCB contain copper pours which complete the switched
current commutation loop (SCCL).
Fig. 2: Two photos of the complete GaN-based inverter.
As shown in fig. 4, the HEMTs are organised into two
straight lines with pin headers connecting to the gate driver
PCB placed right at the gate and source connections of the
HEMTs to minimise gate loop length. Two rows of 18 MLCCs
are used to create a local DC link capacitance of 8.36µF on
the half bridge PCB, close to the HEMTs. An array of 32
100 mil pin headers are used to connect theV DC and PGND
from the motherboard to the half bridge PCB. This method
of connection between the half bridge PCB and motherboard
PCB is chosen to allow easy connection and disconnection
of the power modules, as well as allowing sufficient spacing
between the PCBs to make space for all the parts of the power
module which hang below the half bridge PCB itself. Terminal
blocks are press-fitted into the PCB to act as cable connection
terminals for the switch node of each half bridge.
Fig. 4 shows the top side of the half bridge PCB, including
key sizes and components in the design.
Fig. 5 is a cross-section of the half bridge PCB, including
the two switched current commutation loop pathways.

sink to ensure good thermal contact between the HEMT and

A 10 kΩNTC thermistor is used to monitor the temperature

B. Gate Driver PCB Design
The gate driver PCB is composed of two layers of 1 oz
copper. The top side of the gate driver PCB is used to house
the main bulk of the gate driver components, and the bottom
side is used for the sink and source gate traces.
To encourage symmetric switching of the parallel GaN
HEMTs, the impedances of their gate loops should be matched
as close as possible. To match the gate loop impedances, the
physical layout of the traces is designed such that there is
symmetry in each pathway, similar to designs in previous
publications [24], [25]. Fig. 8 shows the PCB schematic of
the bottom layer of the gate driver PCB. Included are the sink
and source traces and gate resistors, and the gate and source
pin connections, for the high and low side HEMTs. The source
return path is a copper pour on the top layer of the gate driver
PCB, not shown in this figure.
Fig. 8: Bottom layer of the gate driver PCB schematic,
showing the sink and source gate loop traces for the top and
bottom side HEMTs, as well as other key details.
An Infineon 2EDB7259Y is selected to be the gate driver
due to its large current capability of 5 A source and 9 A sink.
The sink and source resistors are selected to keep the peak
sink and source current below the rating of the gate driver.
Because the gate driver has a single output pin for both sinking
and source current, a schottky diode is used to reduce the
impedance of the sink pathway. The selected gate resistances
are 15Ωfor the source gate resistor and 2Ωfor the sink
gate resistor. Pin header connectors are used to individually
connect the gate driver to the gate and source pins of each
HEMT on the half bridge PCB.
Isolated DC/DC Converters are used to provide a bipolar
voltage of +6V/-3V to the top and bottom gate driver channels.
A zener diode is used to reduce the positive voltage from the
power supplies of 6 V down to 5.3 V to increase the safety
margin between the positiveV gs applied to the HEMT and their
data sheet rating. The selection of a bipolar power supply is
to guard against the false turn-on of any of the HEMTs [29],
which could be caused by high dv/dt and parasitics [30] in
the design coupled with the fact that GaN HEMTs have a low
threshold voltage.
Fig. 9 shows the top side of the gate driver PCB.
C. Estimating HEMT Temperature
Figure 4 shows the distance between the thermistor and the
HEMTs on the top side of the half bridge PCB. The thermistor
will be colder than the HEMTs and so using the MATLAB
Partial Differential Equation Toolbox, a steady state thermal
simulation of the system is conducted to model the difference
between the HEMTs junction temperature and the temperature
measured by the thermistor. The HEMTs are modelled as each
generating equal thermal power loss, and it is assumed that all
heat in the system dissipates via the heat sink on top, as this
is the dominant thermal pathway to ambient.

To simplify the geometry of the heat sink, a simplified heat
sink model is used, which is composed of an aluminium plate
of the same thickness as the base of the heat sink, and then a
convective boundary condition with a heat transfer coefficient
chosen to give a resulting thermal resistance equal to the
value given in the heat sinks’ data sheet of 0.9 K/W. This
simplification retains the key thermal characteristics of the heat
sink: The magnitude of the thermal resistance to ambient is
maintained by enforcing a specific heat transfer coefficient at
the boundary, and the horizontal thermal resistance across the
heat sink base.

The test is run for 45 minutes, at an ambient temperature

has been properly designed and implemented for the intended

Running the modules at 155 A, 120 V , gives them a power
rating of 9.3 kW. Given the outer dimensions are 117 x 62
x 59 mm, each module has a volume of 0.43 litres, which
translates to a volumetric power density of 21.7 kW/litre. Each
module weighs 259 g, giving a gravimetric power density of
35.9 kW/kg.
While this volumetric power density is comparatively low,
simple changes to the layout of the half bridge PCB, as well
as increasing the DC link voltage to 140 V , can reduce the
module volume to 0.275 litres, increasing the power density
to 37.1 kW/litre. Excluding the gate driver board, as is done
on many commercial power modules reduces the volume
furthermore down to 0.243 litres, giving a power density of
44.6 kW/litre, comparable to state-of-the-art commercial SiC
power modules when operating under the same electrical and
thermal conditions.

IV. CONCLUSION
This paper presents the design and experimental validation
of a dual, three-phase, GaN-based, inverter with eight parallel
GaN HEMTs per switch, developed for the characterisation
of wide bandgap power electronics in electric vehicle traction
applications. The inverter was successfully operated up to a
steady state phase current of 155 A at 120 V , for switching
frequencies of up to 100 kHz, demonstrating that a high degree
of current sharing between parallel HEMTs can be achieved
through careful gate drive design and symmetrical layout,
as also confirmed by thermal imaging showing comparable
device temperatures across all parallel HEMTs. This indicates
that the design can easily operate at switching frequencies far
above what is achievable with Si devices. Thermal modelling
of the system shows close matching between the measured
thermistor temperature and HEMT junction temperature. Op-
eration of the inverter to drive a three-phase motor was
successful.
Future work will use the inverter and dyno rig to char-
acterise the efficiency of the inverter and motor across a
range of operating conditions, including variations in DC link
voltage, switching frequency, and output filter configuration.
This will provide a more complete understanding of the
system-level benefits and trade-offs associated with the use of
GaN HEMTs at high switching frequencies in electric vehicle
inverter applications.
