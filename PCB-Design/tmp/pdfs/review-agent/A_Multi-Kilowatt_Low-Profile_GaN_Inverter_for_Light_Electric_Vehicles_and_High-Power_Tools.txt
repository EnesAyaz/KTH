A Multi-Kilowatt Low-Profile GaN Inverter for
Light Electric Vehicles and High-Power Tools
Martin Wattenberg, Oscar G. Lorenz, Juan Sanchez
Infineon Technologies Austria AG
Siemensstraße 2, 9500 Villach, Austria
Email: martin.wattenberg@infineon.com
Abstract—An ultra-compact three-phase inverter with 8 de-
vices per switch is presented for a combined output power of 7.3
kW at 48 V . Due to the use of Gallium-Nitride (GaN) transistors
the design can operate at a frequency of 50 kHz and above
without significant loss penalty. Parallel switching of the GaN
device is enabled by symmetrical a layout with a “butterfly”
structure. The high switching frequency enables to shrink the
size of the DC-link capacitor to an overall size of 72 by 132 mm
with a height of as low as 4 mm depending on the chosen cooling
solution resulting in an overall volume of 0.1 liter. The measured
peak efficiency of over 99.3% enables small heatsink size and
high output power with minimal airflow. Under free convection
and device temperature limited to 100°C the inverter reached
an output power of 4 kW with efficiency above 99% across the
operation range. Using active cooling the capable of delivering
7.3 kW while keeping device temperatures around 60°C.
I. I NTRODUCTION
An increasing amount of applications that used to run
off small off-road engines (SORE) are now transitioning to
electric due to environmental concerns as well as legisla-
tive initiatives. Typical applications include larger power and
gardening tools as well as small and light electric vehicles
(LEVs). For these applications there is a strong need for
highly efficient, small and light-weight motor drives to allevi-
ate the increased weight introduced by the batteries. Power
requirement on these motor drives is in the multi-kilowatt
range, i.e. from 2 to 19 kW with a battery voltage ranging
typically from 48 to 120 V . Conventional inverter designs
based on silicon (Si) MOSFET technology struggle to meet
expectation in terms of size and weight [1]. Gallium-Nitride
(GaN) High Electron Mobility Transistors (HEMTs) are a
potentially attractive solution to meet the increased demand
for performance [2]. However, currently these devices are not
widely available in large die sizes which makes it difficult
to address high current application with a single device. To
overcome this issue the literature reports paralleling of up to
four GaN HEMTs successfully [3], [4], [5], [6]. In addition,
several concepts have been proposed to increase the system
output power by paralleling motor drives[7], using poly-phase
[8], [9], [10] or multi-level systems [11], [12]. However, these
solutions come with a lot of added complexity which is not al-
ways desirable. This work presents a high-current three-phase
motor drive with 8 GaN HEMTs per switch in parallel. In
particular, the design is capable of maintaining high switching-
speed and low switching-loss thanks to a consequential low-
impedance design. To the authors knowledge this is the first
comprehensive study on paralleling more than four devices
effectively.
II. F UNDAMENTAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR PARALLELING
ON DEVICE -LEVEL
Similar to Si MOSFETs, GaN HEMTs are majority carrier
devices and exhibit a positive temperature coefficient for
RDS(on) with respect to the junction temperature Tj. For a
100 V Si MOSFET and a comparable GaN HEMT theRDS(on)
vs. Tj is shown in Fig. 1 . The GaN HEMT clearly exhibits
an increase temperature coefficient over the Si MOSFET. For
paralleling this behavior is actually desirable as it helps with
steady-state current balancing. In case of a current imbalance,
the device carrying the most current will heat-up more which
leads to higher resistance and in turn to lower current through
that device.
In addition to the static RDS(on), the transfer character-
istic as well as transconductance ∆ID/∆VGS are important
characteristics for dynamic current sharing. Fig. 2 compares
the behavior for aforementioned devices. For comparison the
plot has been normalized the threshold voltage at 25°C of 3.3
and 2.1 V for the MOSFET and HEMT respectively. At 25°C
the GaN HEMT shows a much higher transconductance than
the MOSFET. However, the transconductance is also more
strongly affected by temperature, effectively slowing down
the switching transient. This temperature coefficient helps
to balance dynamic current sharing, as hotter devices will
naturally carry less of the hard-switching burden than colder
devices.
Furthermore, Fig. 2 provides an indication of the sensitivity
of Vth towards temperature for the MOSFET and HEMT.
While ID remains negligible regardless of temperature for the
HEMT, the MOSFETs drain current increases notably with
temperature when Vth is left at 3.3V . In other words, to keep
the MOSFET at the threshold of conduction, VGS needs to
be decreased considerably with temperature. The temperature
coefficient∆Vth/∆Tj is -3.75 and only -1.1 mV/K for the con-
sidered MOSFET and HEMT respectively. Further information
can be found in a previous publication [13].

III. C ONSIDERATIONS FOR A THREE -PHASE
GAN-O PTIMIZED MOTOR DRIVE
A schematic of the motor drive is given in Fig. 3 . For
simplicity only one of the three half-bridges is shown in detail.
All sense and control signals are exposed on a 32-pin header
with 100 mil pitch to facilitate easy evaluation with different
controllers.
A. Current Sensing for High-Current Applications
Instead of a more conventional low-side shunt resistor, an in-
phase current sense solution based on the TLE4972 differential
Hall sensor was chosen. This provides two significant advan-
tages. First, the power loop for the GaN HEMTs can be opti-
mized by keeping the commutation loops tight and additional
parasitics of the shunt itself can be avoided. Lower parasitics
in the commutation loop reduce ringing in the switch-node
voltage and decrease switching loss. Second, the shunt itself
can introduce non-negligible power loss. With 8 HEMTs in
parallel the effective resistance for this implementation is less
than 400 µΩ. On the one hand a shunt of similar or larger
resistance would introduce a significant loss contribution, thus
nullifying the lower attainable loss by paralleling devices. On
the other hand a significantly smaller shunt in a small package
is technically not trivial and provides extreme challenges in
terms of signal amplification and integrity when scaled back
to the ADCs input range. Hall-based current sensors can easily
overcome these limitations for high-current application. The
chosen TLE4972 is designed for external current rails which
allows to simplify the current flow path from the devices
straight to the screw connector. In order to increase the
intensity of the ⃗B-field, current is concentrated locally beneath
the sensor. This short, narrow section only adds minimal
additional loss and the implementation is shown in Fig. 4. In
this design two layers of 70 µm copper were deemed sufficient.
However, losses can be easily scaled down further by adding
additional layers to the PCB.
B. Temperature Sensing
Junction temperature can be reasonably well estimated by
observing the exposed substrate on the back of the GaN
HEMT with a thermocouple or thermal camera. However,
for situations where the substrate is not easily accessible a
separate temperature sensor was placed in the center of the
board. This sensor is helpful to give an indication of the device
temperatures once a top-side heatsink is applied.

C. Design of Gate Drive Circuit and Layout
An optimized design and layout of the gate driving cir-
cuits is crucial for the performance of the inverter system.
For this design, six Infineon 1EDN7116G EiceDRIVER ICs
were used to drive the high-side and low-side switches of
each phase. This IC includes several important features for
optimally driving GaN SG HEMTs. The truly differential input
(TDI) provides immunity to fast common-mode transients,
including level-shifting for the high-side switch and ground-
bounce avoidance for the low-side. The circuit shown inFig. 3

allows the driver to output a peak current of 4 A for sourcing
and sinking during switching. Furthermore, an active Miller
clamp boost the sinking output to 7 A once the driver detects
that the gate voltage has fallen below 0.4 V . This feature
avoids spurious switching due to the Miller effect. Added
robustness against induced turn-on is provided by an integrated
charge pump within the driver, which internally generates
an adjustable negative rail to ensure that all GaN HEMTs
remain off at the proper times [14]. It is important to consider
that GaN HEMTs have a reverse conduction mechanism that
mimics a MOSFET body diode, with the exception that the
"body diode" voltage drop is directly proportional to the neg-
ative voltage applied. Therefore, applying a more negative of-
state voltage than necessary produces higher losses during the
dead-time. Furthermore, when the high-side driver is supplied
via bootstrapping as in Fig. 3 this mechanism increases the
effective voltage being applied during deadtime. A Zener
diode (D ZNR) is therefore included in parallel to C VDD of
the high-side. The amount of over-charging will depend on
the (parasitic) impedance and deadtime.
The high-side gate traces are shown in Fig. 4. Great care
was given to the symmetrical design of the gate loops via
the “butterfly” structure with the gate driver in the center.
Implementation of the low-side traces is similar. Moreover,
this layer also contains part of the switch-node for which
the output current is indicated. A second switch-node layer
is placed directly on top providing a tightly coupled, low-
inductance return loop for the gate current as well as lower
resistance for the output current.

D. Sizing of the DC-Link
As [15] has shown, the RMS current of the DC-link in
a voltage-source-inverter is primarily dependent on the output
current of the inverter and power factor. As first approximation
for sizing the DC-link, the capacitor RMS current ICin can be
derived from the phase current Iphs as
ICin = 0.65 · Iphs. (1)
For a targeted 130 Arms this results in about 85 Arms current
going through the DC-link. For a single capacitor this would
be considerable. However, thanks to the low switching-loss
of GaN HEMTs, frequency can be increased to a point
where MLCCs become viable instead of aluminum electrolyte
capacitors. With respect to their size the current handling
capability of MLCCs is much higher and paralleling them
is straightforward. Paralleling many MLCCs has the added
benefit of reducing the parasitic inductance, thus reducing
ringing and improving current sharing [13].
In addition to the current handling capability, the voltage
ripple needs to be considered. The amount of total DC-
link capacitance CDCdepends on the acceptable voltage ripple

solution is required. For this reason all GaN devices have
been placed placed on one side of the PCB to facilitate easy

attachment to a heatsink. Crucially, all components have been
chosen not to exceed the height of the HEMTs. The dual-sided

IV. I MPLEMENTED PROTOTYPE
The prototype is implemented on a six-layer PCB with
70 µm of copper on each layer and a total size of 132 by
72 mm. Each half-bridge consist of 8 Infineon CoolGaN™
100 V 3 mΩ HEMTs in parallel for the low-side switch as
well 8 devices in parallel for the high-side switch. Each
switch is driven by an Infineon EiceDRIVER™ 1EDN7116G.
Current sensing is done through in-phase sensing based on the
Infineon XENSIV™ TLE4972 magnetic current sensor. The
inverter is controlled by an Infineon XMC™ 4400 industrial
microcontroller with 150 ps high-resolution PWM for precise
control.
Fig. 6 shows a close-up image of a single half-bridge. The
active components (HEMTs, gate driver etc.) occupy an area
of 30 by 25 mm and the total size of the half-bridge incl.
DC-link is 47 by 37 mm. A detailed description of the layer
stack-up can be found in [13].
The total DC-link of 580 µF is split up across the three
phases. Each phase contains 87 MLCCs in 1206 with 2.2 µF
each plus an additional 42 MLCCs in 0603 with 100 nF each
for the high frequency switching current and are located at the
top and bottom of the half-bridge. The nominal capacitance of
the DC-link is 580 µF but care must be taken to account for
DC-bias. Therefore, additional footprints for leaded capacitors
are also provisioned.
Components on either side of the PCB are 1 mm or less
resulting in an overall thickness of less than 4 mm. Several
mounting holes can be seen which allow either off-the-shelve
heatsinks to be mounted or the entire PCB can be attached to
a cold plate. This can also provide additional paths for heat
to leave the PCB via the MLCCs.
The negative VGS of the gate driver was limited to -1.5 V
providing generous margin against parasitic turn-on. In paral-
leled designs it is often common-practice to place individual
gate resistors in front of each gate to dampen oscillations
caused by the Miller charge. For this design RG was specified
at 1Ω with no observable degradation in switching speed. The
optional additional CGS was chosen to be 270 pF. Further
refinements to the negative VGS, R G and CGS are possible
depending on application requirements.
A 5.1 V Zener diode was used on the high-side to limit over-
charging. To further limit the gate current Rdrv was chosen to
be 1Ω.
Fig. 7 shows the full PCB with three push-pin heatsinks with
30 by 30 mm size and a fin height of 10 mm. Because the top-

its source terminal, an electrically isolated thermal interface

When attaching the entire top-side of the PCB to a custom

V. E XPERIMENTAL VERIFICATION
Verifying correct operation of 8 paralleled devices in a
three-phase system is not trivial. To facilitate experimental
evaluation the converter, it is first operated as a simple
buck converter with 50% duty cycle. This makes efficiency
measurements straightforward and the switching behavior for
individual current levels can be analyzed more easily.
In order to verify correct operation of all 8 devices in
parallel, thermal imaging is used. In a previous work current
sharing between the individual half-bridges was analyzed and
only showed minor variations [13].
Probing and waveform acquisition was done with a 1 GHz
passive probe and scope with equal bandwidth and 6.25 GS/s.
For ease of use during initial testing, probes with MMCX tips
were used. These allow to quickly connect to different signals.
The PCB exposes important signals on dedicated test pads to
which short, twisted wires were soldered onto, see left side of
Fig. 6 and bottom side of Fig. 7. High-side VGS measurements
use the same connection scheme with an optically isolated
probe with 800 MHz bandwidth. Even though this setup is
highly convenient, it was found that some noise can still couple
into the twisted wires. Especially for small signals such asVGS
and in particular for the floating high-side VGS this must be
considered.
A. Operation As a Buck Converter
To confirm safe voltage levels of the high-side driver, VDD
was measured with an isolated multimeter at 50 kHz, 25 ns
deadtime and a negative VGS of -1.5 V . Fig. 8 shows VDD
in relation to the output current up to 130 A at 48 V . With
increasing output current the “diode-like” operation of the
GaN device gets more and more pronounced which leads to an
increasing voltage being applied to the high-side driver during
deadtime. However, as long as VGS stays within acceptable
limits this behavior is beneficial by providing more symmet-
rical supply voltages between high and low-side. Under the
considered conditions the high-side VDD reaches about 4.77 V
at 130 A which almost fully compensates for the bootstrap
diode drop while staying within recommend voltage levels.
Higher deadtime value lead quickly to over-charging the high-
side VDD and excessive power loss in the Zener diode (not
shown).
Fig. 9 and Fig. 10 show the falling and rising edge of the
switch-node voltage Vsw respectively. Due to the operation as
a sync. buck, all falling edges are either partial or full ZVS
transitions while rising edges are (partially) hard switching.
The switch-node probing point was attached to the middle
of the switch-node, thus giving an indication of the overall
switching behavior instead of one device specifically.VGS was
measured for the high-side device Q9 and the low-side device
Q10 as close as possible on test points directly beneath the
devices (c.f. Fig. 6 for designators and device placement).
Fig. 9 shows a set of waveforms ranging from 0 to 130 A
output current with little ringing on all waveforms, due to the
soft ZVS transition. At 130 A the slew rate reaches 13 V/ns.

B. Operation as a Three Phase Motor Drive
After correct operation of a single half-bridge has been
confirmed this section covers operation as a three phase
inverter.
For accurate efficiency measurement, a passive inductive-
restive load was chosen to emulate a full motor system.Fig. 13
shows the inverter efficiency versus RMS output current for
different cooling solutions when operated at 48 V with 50 kHz
switching frequency and 25 ns deadtime. Peak temperature was
limited to 100°C in all experiments based on the on-board
sensor.
In free air with natural convection an output current per
phase of 67 A rms was achieved with measured inverter loss
of 25 W. At maximum modulation index with space vector
modulation this represents a total output power of 4 kW a
restive load. In the final application output power can be
slightly less, depending on the amount of reactive power (i.e.
cos(ϕ)) the inverter has to support at the motor side. Output
current can be increased to 73 Arms or 4.3 kW when using the
small, off-the-shelve push-pin heatsinks shown in Fig. 7. Due

VI. C ONCLUSION
This work presents an ultra-compact three-phase inverter
with eight 100 V CoolGaN™ Schottky Gate HEMTs in paral-
lel per switch position. Due to the temperature coefficients
of the GaN HEMT, paralleling a large number of devices
is fundamentally no more difficult than with Si MOSFETs.
Balanced gate and power-loops are ensured by the presented
highly symmetrical “butterfly” layout. Furthermore, design
considerations for the gate driver and DC-link are presented
to fully utilize the benefits of the HEMTs in a motor drive
application.

The implemented PCB has a total size of 132 by 72 mm with
a maximum thickness of 4 mm. Using natural convection and
no heatsink, this configuration achieves 4 kW of output power
at 48 V while keeping efficiency above 99%. With moderate
airflow of 4 m/s the output power can be increased to 6 kW.
Adding a heatsink with 10 mm height enabled the output
power to reach 6.6 kW with the same 4 m/s forced air, and up
to 7.3 kW with 10 m/s air flow. Preliminary testing indicates
much higher power levels are feasible, especially when the
PCB is mounted to a coldplate via screws for increased
compression of the TIM and lower thermal resistance. All
device temperatures stayed within expectation, no abnormal
temperature distribution could be detected.
This work has shown how a GaN-based motor drive offers
very high efficiency across a wide power range, making it
clearly very attractive for electrification of applications that
currently use a conventional combustion engine. Lower weight
and smaller size add further value to applications like handheld
high-power tools, as well as increasing the range of light
electric vehicles.
