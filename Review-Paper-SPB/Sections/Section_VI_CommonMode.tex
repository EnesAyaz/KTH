\section{Common-Mode Voltage Effects}
\label{sec:common_mode}
% ============================================================================

In any converter-fed ac machine, the non-zero average of the three
phase-leg pole voltages produced by PWM switching gives rise to a
common-mode voltage (CMV) that excites parasitic capacitive paths
between winding, frame, shaft, and bearings. This mechanism is well
characterized for a converter with a single machine port whose voltage
scaling is produced internally to the converter, such as the
flying-capacitor multilevel converter, for which an analytical CM-EMI
model referenced to one dc-link potential is available
\cite{Giardine2025FCMLEMI}. SPB does not fit this picture. Because its
$N$ converter cells are connected in series on the dc side
(Section~\ref{sec:operating_principle}) while each cell feeds an
electrically separate winding group, the local dc rails of cell $k$
are displaced from the overall dc-bus reference by an amount that
depends on the cell's position in the series stack. The CMV problem in
SPB is therefore not $N$ independent copies of the single-inverter
case: it is $N$ common-mode sources referenced to $N$ different
absolute potentials, coupled through a shared machine frame, shaft,
and cooling structure.

\subsection{Series-Stack Origin of Common-Mode Excitation}

Taking cell 1's local negative dc rail as the reference and stacking
the remaining cells so that cell $k$'s negative rail coincides with
cell $(k-1)$'s positive rail, the absolute offset of cell $k$ is

\begin{equation}
V_{-,k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}V_{\mathrm{dc},j}
\approx(k-1)\frac{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}{N},
\label{eq:spb_cm_offset}
\end{equation}

using the voltage-partitioning relation \eqref{eq:spb_voltage_sum}. The
common-mode potential seen by winding group $k$ relative to this fixed
reference is then

\begin{equation}
v_{\mathrm{cm},k}^{\mathrm{abs}}(t)=
V_{-,k}+\frac{1}{3}\left(v_{aN_k}+v_{bN_k}+v_{cN_k}\right),
\label{eq:spb_cm_local}
\end{equation}

where $v_{aN_k},v_{bN_k},v_{cN_k}$ are the pole voltages of cell $k$
referenced to its own local negative rail $N_k$. The second term is
the conventional switching-induced CMV already addressed by
zero-sequence injection and vector-redundancy modulation
(Section~\ref{sec:modulation}); the first term, $V_{-,k}$, is specific
to SPB and is set by the cell's position in the stack and by the
instantaneous voltage-sharing state discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:dclink_balancing}, not by modulation alone.

Fig.~\ref{fig:spb_cm_stack} illustrates the resulting structure:
increasing absolute potential offset with stack position, together
with representative common-mode current paths coupling each winding
group to the shared machine frame and chassis ground through parasitic
capacitance. Because the converter cells and their winding groups are
physically close together in the integrated implementations discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:machine_requirements}, these parasitic paths are
short and the electromagnetic coupling between cells is direct rather
than mediated by long cabling. Physically integrated
motor-drive assemblies show that common-mode current can resonate
through exactly this kind of parasitic loop, and that the resulting
resonance is not adequately predicted by a single-source CM model
\cite{Zhong2025CMResonance,Chen2020EMCIntegrated}. Extending such
modeling from a single CM source to a multi-port structure with $N$
distinct dc offsets is, to the authors' knowledge, not yet available
for SPB specifically -- the gap left open by the single-port
flying-capacitor comparison drawn above \cite{Giardine2025FCMLEMI}.

For the special case $N=2$, an SPB-specific mitigation option exists
that is structurally different from the stack-offset mechanism above.
If cell 2 is driven with the logical inverse of cell 1's gate
commands, and cell 2's winding set is wired with reversed winding
sense so that both cells still generate torque in the same direction,
the two cells' individually switched common-mode voltages become
approximately equal and opposite, $v_{\mathrm{cm},1}(t)\approx
-v_{\mathrm{cm},2}(t)$, and the total common-mode voltage seen by the
machine,
\begin{equation}
v_{\mathrm{cm,tot}}(t)=\frac{v_{\mathrm{cm},1}(t)+v_{\mathrm{cm},2}(t)}{2},
\label{eq:spb_cm_cancellation}
\end{equation}
cancels toward zero instead of adding constructively. This
balanced-cancellation principle was first demonstrated for a
dual-winding-set machine driven by a balanced inverter pair on a
single, non-series-stacked machine \cite{Han2018CMCancellation}, and
was later applied to a genuine two-cell series-stacked (2L-SSC) SPB
inverter \cite{Rohner2024GaN}; as already noted for interleaved GaN
IMMDs, the achievable CMV reduction is likewise ultimately limited by
layout parasitics and local dc-link capacitor design
\cite{Wang2015GaNIMMD}.
This cancellation assumes perfectly matched, simultaneous switching
transitions between the two cells; a propagation-delay mismatch
between the two cells' gate signals (e.g., from PCB layout or driver
differences) reintroduces a residual common-mode spike, of duration
equal to the delay, at every misaligned switching transition, and
unequal power sharing between the two cells during dc-link balancing
(Section~\ref{sec:dclink_balancing}) similarly degrades the
cancellation, both shown quantitatively in
\cite{Rohner2024GaN,Han2018CMCancellation}. This
pairwise-cancellation mechanism is specific to $N=2$ (or to a stack of
matched cell pairs) and does not generalize to arbitrary $N$, so it
should be read as a valuable special-case mitigation option rather
than a general solution to the stack-offset common-mode problem
discussed above. Fig.~\ref{fig:spb_cm_cancellation} simulates this
cancellation mechanism directly from switching-function waveforms,
including its effect on the resulting bearing/ground current.

\subsection{Physical Consequences and Mitigation}

CMV-driven bearing current is a documented failure mechanism in
inverter-fed machines generally, arising from capacitive coupling
through the air gap and from discharge events once the bearing
lubricant film breaks down \cite{Lee2025BearingCurrent}, and it can be
identified with dedicated non-intrusive current-measurement techniques
\cite{Li2025BearingMeasurement}. In SPB, this mechanism is present at
each of the $N$ winding groups individually, and because
\eqref{eq:spb_cm_offset}--\eqref{eq:spb_cm_local} gives each winding
group a different absolute CM reference, the resulting bearing- and
shaft-voltage stress is not necessarily uniform across the stack.
Whether it is dominated by the highest-offset cell, by the combined
contribution of all cells, or by resonant interaction between cells is
an open question that the direct SPB literature has not yet addressed
quantitatively. Shaft-voltage buildup and associated common-mode noise
are also targeted directly by cancellation-based circuits developed
for conventional drives \cite{Xie2026ShaftCM}; such circuits are a
plausible mitigation candidate for SPB but have not been demonstrated
for a series-stacked, multi-port machine.

The same switching events that generate CMV also produce fast
$dv/dt$ transitions at the winding terminals, which stress
turn-to-turn and phase-to-frame insulation independently of the CM
current path itself. WBG devices, already motivated for SPB by their
favorable scaling at reduced blocking voltage
(Section~\ref{sec:semiconductor_scaling}), shorten rise time and
increase overshoot, and have been shown to accelerate insulation aging
and partial-discharge activity in hairpin and other traction-machine
windings \cite{KholghKhiz2025HairpinWBG,Gulec2025PDImpulse,
Seri2025ATFPDIV}; frequency-response and other diagnostic methods have
been developed to assess the resulting insulation condition
\cite{Kim2025HairpinFRA}, within the broader set of insulation
technologies available for transport-electrification machines
\cite{Martinez2025TransportInsulation}. In SPB, this $dv/dt$ stress is
superimposed on the stack-position-dependent offset of
\eqref{eq:spb_cm_offset}, so insulation coordination must account for
both the local switching transition and the cell's absolute position
in the series stack, consistent with the insulation challenge already
identified qualitatively in Section~\ref{sec:machine_requirements}.

Mitigation therefore has to act at more than one level. Active EMI
filtering can attenuate common-mode current at the converter terminals
without relying on machine or bearing hardware
\cite{Ihsan2025ActiveEMI}, and cancellation circuits can target shaft
voltage directly \cite{Xie2026ShaftCM}. At the modulation level,
Section~\ref{sec:modulation} already showed that zero-sequence
injection and redundant-vector selection can reduce CMV excitation
\cite{Sun2025CMVMPC,Zheng2025MultiObjectivePWM}. However, the same
section also showed that the carrier-phase displacement used to
interleave switching harmonics for dc-link and machine ripple
reduction, \eqref{eq:carrier_phase}--\eqref{eq:harmonic_phasor_sum}, is
chosen to cancel a selected ripple harmonic and is not simultaneously
optimized for common-mode excitation. Because SPB's CM problem
additionally depends on the fixed stack offset $V_{-,k}$, which carrier
phasing cannot change, an interleaving choice that minimizes dc-link or
stator current ripple need not minimize aggregate common-mode
excitation across the stack, and vice versa. This is a genuine
SPB-specific design tension: the multi-objective modulation problem
identified in Section~\ref{sec:modulation} should be extended, in
future SPB-specific studies, to include common-mode and
insulation-stress metrics evaluated across the full stack rather than
at a single representative cell.
