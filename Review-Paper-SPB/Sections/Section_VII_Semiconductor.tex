\section{Semiconductor Technology and Cell-Count Scaling}
\label{sec:semiconductor_scaling}
% ============================================================================

Increasing the SPB cell count $N$ enables lower-voltage semiconductor
devices, but voltage-rating selection must account for maximum blocking
stress:
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{rated}}
\geq
k_{\mathrm{rel}}
\left(
\frac{V_{\mathrm{dc,max}}}{N}
+\Delta V_{\mathrm{imb}}^{\max}
+\Delta V_{\mathrm{sw}}^{\max}
\right),
\label{eq:semiconductor_rating}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta V_{\mathrm{imb}}^{\max}$ is the maximum cell-voltage
imbalance, $\Delta V_{\mathrm{sw}}^{\max}$ is the commutation overshoot,
and $k_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is the selected voltage-rating margin factor.
Cell-count selection therefore depends on discrete commercial voltage
classes and their usable operating margins.

SiC is well suited to high-voltage traction, while voltage partitioning
can make lower-voltage GaN devices attractive through favorable
conduction and switching characteristics
\cite{WBGTractionReview2025,Rohner2024GaN,Ebrahimian2025GaNIMMD}.
For the illustrative 1200-V dc link in Fig.~\ref{fig:cell_count_scaling},
two cells operate at a nominal 600~V each, leaving limited headroom for
650-V devices. Three cells reduce this voltage to 400~V, providing
greater allowance for imbalance and switching transients. Further
increases in $N$ enable lower-voltage device classes but increase the
number of active switch positions as $6N$. Final device selection must
satisfy \eqref{eq:semiconductor_rating}.

\subsection{Commercial-Device Screening}

Lower voltage ratings do not guarantee superior converter performance.
Candidate devices must be assessed using conduction and switching
characteristics together with current capability, thermal limits, and
packaging constraints. Figures of merit such as
$R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}C_{\mathrm{oss}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}Q_g$
provide initial screening, but detailed comparison requires
$R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}(T)$ for conduction loss and die-area requirements;
$E_{\mathrm{oss}}(V)$ for stored output-capacitance energy and its
contribution to switching loss; $E_{\mathrm{on/off}}(V,I,T)$ for
switching-energy characterization; $Q_g$ and $Q_{gd}$ for gate-drive
requirements and switching behavior; $I_{\mathrm{cont}}$ and $R_\theta$
for current derating, parallelization, and cooling; and package and
power-loop inductance $L_\sigma$ for commutation overshoot.

Machine phase current does not generally decrease as $1/N$. For a
derated device current capability $I_{\mathrm{dev,der}}$, the required
parallel-device count and total active-device count can be estimated as
\begin{equation}
n_p
=
\left\lceil
\frac{I_{\mathrm{sw,max}}}
     {I_{\mathrm{dev,der}}}
\right\rceil,
\qquad
N_{\mathrm{sw,tot}}
=
6Nn_p .
\label{eq:parallel_device_scaling}
\end{equation}
Consequently, favorable device-level figures of merit may coexist with
substantial die area, package count, and gate-drive and thermal-interface
requirements. High-current GaN modules illustrate the importance of
low-inductance packaging and integrated gate driving at traction-relevant
currents \cite{Zhu2024GaN150A,Rahman2025GaN300A}.

Device selection and layout are further coupled through commutation
overshoot:
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{\mathrm{sw}}
\approx
L_{\sigma}\frac{di}{dt},
\label{eq:semiconductor_overshoot}
\end{equation}
Package and interconnection inductance can consume much of the voltage
margin gained through partitioning. High switching speeds therefore
require careful commutation-loop design, local decoupling, and
gate-drive layout, particularly for GaN \cite{Rahman2025GaN300A}.
Dynamic $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$, reverse conduction, gate reliability,
short-circuit capability, and thermal performance must also be
considered \cite{Ajayan2025GaNReliability,Ajayan2025GaNFabrication}.
Room-temperature static resistance alone is therefore insufficient for
cell-count selection.

\subsection{Loss Scaling With Cell Count}

For an individual device, conduction and switching losses can be
approximated as
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{cond}}
\approx
I_{\mathrm{rms,sw}}^2
R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}(T_j),
\label{eq:cond_loss_short}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{sw}}
\approx
f_{\mathrm{sw}}
\left[
E_{\mathrm{on}}(V,I,T_j)
+
E_{\mathrm{off}}(V,I,T_j)
\right].
\label{eq:switching_loss_short}
\end{equation}
Including dead-time-related losses and gate-drive power gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{semi}}(N)
={}&\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{6n_p(N)}
\bigl(P_{\mathrm{cond},k,j}+P_{\mathrm{sw},k,j}
+P_{\mathrm{dt},k,j}\bigr)\\
&+P_{\mathrm{gate}}(N),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:complete_semiconductor_loss}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{dt},k,j}$ represents dead-time-related device loss and
$P_{\mathrm{gate}}$ is the aggregate gate-drive power.

Total loss does not scale linearly with $N$: additional cells increase
device count, while transitions between voltage classes change on-state
resistance, switching energy, thermal impedance, and parallelization
requirements. Low-cell-count designs may favor high-voltage SiC,
whereas lower-voltage GaN becomes attractive when its device, packaging,
or passive-component benefits offset the additional modules.

Cell count and semiconductor technology must therefore be selected
jointly using the voltage and component-count relationships in
Fig.~\ref{fig:cell_count_scaling}, commercial-device characteristics,
and the machine requirements of Section~\ref{sec:machine_requirements}.
No single device figure of merit establishes the preferred SPB architecture.
