\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================================

Electrification is pushing traction-drive propulsion and fast-charging
power toward several hundred kilowatts; meanwhile, system-level performance
requirements continue to rise \cite{EVTractionReview2023}. At that point, increasing the dc-link voltage has emerged as a primary design lever for meeting
both demands. On the propulsion side, a higher dc-link voltage
can improve efficiency and power density \cite{DriveCodesign800V2023}. On
the battery and charging side, it reduces the current required for a
given power level, lowering cable conduction loss and conductor
cross-section while enabling higher charging power under a given
current limit \cite{Post800V2026}.

This trend, however, is not without consequence at the converter
level. A higher dc-link voltage also brings more severe
insulation-coordination and electromagnetic-compatibility (EMC)
demands, together with added thermal and reliability stress on the
power stage \cite{DriveCodesign800V2023,Post800V2026}.
Raising the dc-link voltage therefore requires coordinated converter,
machine, and thermal design.

Figure~\ref{fig:intro_hv_motivation} summarizes these qualitative
tradeoffs between conventional low-voltage and higher-voltage traction
architectures.

\subsection{Literature and Industry Trends Toward Higher DC-Link Voltage}

The trend motivated above is not merely conceptual: it is already
established in academic prototypes, industrial inverter platforms, and
production vehicles, as summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:intro_voltage_power}. Reported academic demonstrators
range from 400-V to 800-V and 1.2-kV converters, with still higher
levels investigated for high-power passenger and heavy-duty
applications \cite{Post800V2026}, while industrial platforms
increasingly target 800-V operation as a baseline and production
vehicles show a comparable shift from established 400-V architectures
to a growing number of 800-V and higher-voltage platforms
\cite{Aghabali2021TTE800V,Mousavi2025ICTEM800V}. This shift makes semiconductor blocking-voltage capability a central
converter-design constraint.

\subsection{Semiconductor Blocking-Voltage Tradeoff}

\label{sec:semiconductor_limitations}

In a conventional two-level voltage-source inverter, each active
switch must block a voltage directly tied to the complete dc-link
voltage. In unipolar power devices, a higher blocking-voltage rating
imposes a fundamental tradeoff against both specific on-state
resistance and switching performance, commonly captured by the device figure-of-merit (D-FOM), as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_ronsp_trend}
\cite{Millan2014WBGSurvey,SpecificResistance}. This ideal scaling favors lower blocking voltage within a given
material model; commercial-device comparisons must also account for
structure, temperature, packaging, and switching conditions.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=210.933bp]{Figures/Exported/fig02.pdf}
\caption{Ideal one-dimensional unipolar specific on-state
    resistance $R_{\mathrm{on,sp}}$ (solid, left axis) and the
    resulting high-frequency device figure-of-merit
    $\text{D-FOM}=1/\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{on,sp}}C_{\mathrm{oss,sp}}}$
    (dashed, right axis), in $\sqrt{\mathrm{MHz}}$ -- the product
    $R_{\mathrm{on,sp}}C_{\mathrm{oss,sp}}$ is an area-independent $RC$
    time constant, versus blocking voltage for Si, 4H-SiC, and GaN.}
    \label{fig:intro_ronsp_trend}
\end{figure}

Furthermore, wide-bandgap (WBG) semiconductors shift this D-FOM favorably: SiC
metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) now
dominate high-voltage automotive drives, while GaN is particularly
attractive at lower voltage owing to its fast switching behavior and
superior D-FOM \cite{WideReview2018,WBGTractionReview2025,GaNComparison,
Millan2014WBGSurvey}.
Yet switching material does not remove the
underlying coupling between dc-link voltage and device voltage class:
a higher dc-link voltage can require a higher device voltage class,
with conduction and switching losses determined by the selected
technology and operating conditions.
At this point, converter architectures that partition the dc-link voltage among multiple
switching units, so each device blocks only a fraction of the total
voltage, therefore become increasingly relevant as system voltage
rises.

\subsection{Multilevel Converters and Their Limitations}

Multilevel converters provide a way to partition voltage and utilize
lower-voltage devices.
NPC, T-type, flying-capacitor, and cascaded architectures all distribute
the dc-link voltage among
additional semiconductor positions or capacitor-voltage levels,
reducing individual device voltage stress
\cite{Poorfakhraei2021MultilevelReview,T_type_SiC,
FlyingCap_Optimized3D,CascadedHBridge_control}.

They require additional semiconductor positions, passive components,
and gate drivers, which increases the number of potential failure points. Classical three-phase multilevel
bridges still supply a common winding set, whereas cascaded and other
modular architectures can provide module-level redundancy. In either
case, fault tolerance depends on available isolation paths, spare
voltage capability, and control; component count alone establishes
neither fault tolerance nor system reliability.

\subsection{Stacked Polyphase Bridge}
\label{sec:spb_modularity}

The stacked polyphase bridge (SPB) distributes the
high-voltage conversion function among $N$ repeated converter units.
Each unit is a conventional three-phase two-level bridge connected in
series on the dc side, supplying its own electrically separated
three-phase winding group of the same machine, as illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:spb_architecture}.

Under balanced operation, each cell's local dc-link voltage
$V_{\mathrm{dc},k}\approx V_{\mathrm{dc}}/N$, so the semiconductor
voltage rating can be selected on the local cell voltage rather than
the complete traction dc-link voltage -- addressing the semiconductor
bottleneck much as multilevel converters do. Each cell also drives a separated winding group, providing
modularity at the converter--machine interface.
A faulted cell can, in principle, be isolated and bypassed while the
remaining cells continue to drive the machine at reduced power. It is
this combination of semiconductor voltage reduction and
converter--machine modularity that distinguishes SPB from conventional
multilevel and cascaded architectures and motivates its consideration
as a candidate topology for next-generation traction drives.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=240.267bp]{Figures/Exported/fig03.pdf}
\caption{Conceptual architecture of an $N$-cell SPB traction
    drive: series-connected local dc-link capacitors, series-connected
    three-phase two-level bridge cells, and their electrically
    separated machine winding groups.}
    \label{fig:spb_architecture}
\end{figure}

Realizing this potential, however, introduces coupled design
challenges in component count, dc-link dynamics, machine design,
common-mode voltage, and fault handling, addressed throughout this
review. These challenges have grown more tractable as neighboring
research areas have matured: multiphase machines provide additional
post-fault degrees of freedom, integrated modular motor drives show
how repeated power-electronic units can be physically associated with
individual machine sections, wide-bandgap semiconductors amplify
performance differences among voltage classes, and distributed
control coordinates modular converter units
\cite{Choi2011IMMD,Wang2025GaNIMMD,
JahnsDai2017IMDReview,Bringezu2020SPB,Deng2026IMDReview,
Ebrahimian2025GaNIMMD}.

Despite more than a decade of SPB research, beginning with Norrga
\textit{et al.} \cite{Norrga2013SPB}, the feasibility of electric-vehicle traction applications requires
these developments to be assessed together. The available literature
remains distributed across converter topology, modulation, dc-link
stability, multiphase-machine design, integrated motor drives,
semiconductor technology, thermal management, distributed control, and
fault-tolerant operation. Although these topics are strongly coupled
in an SPB traction drive, they are rarely evaluated within a common
system boundary, leaving a gap between a well-motivated architectural
concept and a system-level feasibility assessment. Accordingly, this
paper provides a system-level review of SPB traction-drive technology
aimed at closing that gap.

\subsection{Review Scope and Contribution}
This is a focused narrative review rather than an exhaustive systematic
survey or a meta-analysis. Direct SPB evidence concerns series-connected
dc ports supplying electrically separated winding groups; related
multiphase and integrated-drive studies provide enabling methods but
are not treated as demonstrations of SPB traction performance. The
review distinguishes reported results, analytical illustrations, and
application-level inferences. Its contribution is to connect device
voltage partitioning to winding feasibility, modulation, balancing,
common-mode excitation, and post-fault operation within one EV design
boundary.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, SPB's
historical development, operating principle, and modulation are
consolidated. Second, the interaction between cell count and the
electric machine is examined alongside multiphase-machine and
integrated-modular-drive research. Third, common-mode voltage and
dc-link dynamics and balancing are examined. Fourth, semiconductor
voltage scaling and fault handling are treated as coupled
architecture-level design problems. Finally, the remaining research
gaps and automotive validation requirements are identified.
