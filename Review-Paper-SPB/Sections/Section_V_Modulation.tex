% ============================================================================
\section{Modulation Strategies}
\label{sec:modulation}
% ============================================================================

Each SPB cell is electrically simple, a conventional two-level bridge
(Sections~\ref{sec:operating_principle}
and~\ref{sec:machine_requirements}), while the resulting control
problem is not: cell dc current is shared, cell dc voltage is a
dynamic state, and the winding groups are electromagnetically coupled.
Modulation is the layer at which these converter- and machine-side
constraints are jointly realized in real time.

Because each SPB cell is a conventional three-phase two-level bridge,
standard SPWM, SVM, and DPWM methods can be applied locally without
modification. The distinctive feature of SPB is that modulation can
additionally be coordinated \emph{between} cells through carrier
phase, zero-sequence injection, switching sequence, and clamping
intervals \cite{Jin2014Control,Jin2017Modulation}. These inter-cell
degrees of freedom affect dc-link ripple, machine harmonics,
semiconductor switching loss, common-mode voltage (CMV), and the power
exchanged by the individual winding groups, and they have no
counterpart in a single conventional three-phase inverter.

\subsection{Carrier Coordination and Harmonic Cancellation}

For carrier-based PWM, uniform interleaving sets

\begin{equation}
\phi_{c,k}=\phi_{c,1}+(k-1)\Delta\phi_c,
\qquad
\Delta\phi_c=\frac{2\pi}{N}.
\label{eq:carrier_phase}
\end{equation}

A switching harmonic of similar amplitude in all cells then combines
approximately as

\begin{equation}
I_{h,\Sigma}=I_h\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{jhk\Delta\phi_c},
\label{eq:harmonic_phasor_sum}
\end{equation}

so that an appropriate phase displacement can suppress selected
switching-frequency components, as illustrated for the $N=3$ case
discussed throughout this subsection. SPB-specific studies have shown
that
inter-cell modulation affects semiconductor losses and dc-link-current
harmonics \cite{Jin2017Modulation}; related modular and multiphase
drives further demonstrate simultaneous reduction of dc-link and
machine current ripple \cite{Verkroost2021Ripple,
DeMarcos2025InterleavedPWM,Wang2025HybridPWM}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic_phasor_sum} treats each cell's ripple
contribution as a single tone at the switching frequency; a rigorous
double-Fourier-series analysis of the actual switched converter
current gives the exact survival condition instead of this single-tone
approximation. Writing the switching function of phase leg $x$ in cell
$k$ in the usual double-Fourier form, with carrier index $m$ (at
carrier frequency $\omega_{c,k}$, phase $\theta_{c,k}$) and sideband
index $n$ (at fundamental frequency $\omega_{0,k}$, phase
$\theta_{0,k}$),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{x,k}(t)={}&\frac{A_{00}}{2}
+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big[A_{0n}\cos(n[\omega_{0,k}t+\theta_{0,k}])
+B_{0n}\sin(\cdot)\big]\\
&+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\big[A_{m0}\cos(m[\omega_{c,k}t+\theta_{c,k}])
+B_{m0}\sin(\cdot)\big]\\
&+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\substack{n=-\infty\\n\neq0}}^{\infty}
\Big[A_{mn}\cos\big(m[\omega_{c,k}t+\theta_{c,k}]\\
&\hspace{4.7em}+n[\omega_{0,k}t+\theta_{0,k}]\big)
+B_{mn}\sin(\cdot)\Big],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dfs_switching}
\end{equation}
the cell's converter-side current follows from convolving each phase's
switching-function spectrum with its current spectrum in the frequency
domain, $I_{\mathrm{conv},x,k}=\tfrac12(S_{x,k}\otimes I_{x,k})$, and
summing over the three phases \cite{Jin2014Control}. For a single
cell, this shows every carrier harmonic $m$ contributes sidebands at
$n=\pm6p-3$ ($m$ odd) or $n=\pm6p$ ($m$ even), for integer $p$ --
richer than the single-tone picture of \eqref{eq:harmonic_phasor_sum}.
With uniform interleaving $\theta_{c,k}=2\pi k/N$, summing the $N$
cells' converter currents shows that only carrier harmonics $m$ that
are themselves multiples of $N$ survive ($m=pN$ for even $N$, $m=2pN$
for odd $N$), each retaining the same $n=\pm6p-3$/$\pm6p$ sideband
structure \cite{Jin2014Control}. This confirms, from the complete
switched-current spectrum rather than a single-tone approximation,
that the cancellation condition of \eqref{eq:carrier_phase} extends to
the full family of sidebands around each carrier harmonic, not just
its fundamental tone.

The same reference derives the resulting dc-side current directly from
the SPB equivalent circuit already used in
Sections~\ref{sec:operating_principle}
and~\ref{sec:dclink_balancing} -- $L\,di_{\mathrm{dc}}/dt=E-Ri_{\mathrm{dc}}
-\sum_kv_{\mathrm{conv},k}$, $C_k\,dv_{\mathrm{conv},k}/dt=i_{\mathrm{dc}}
-i_{\mathrm{conv},k}$, the same governing equations underlying the
coupled circuit simulation of
Fig.~\ref{fig:dclink_ripple_interleaving} -- giving a closed-form
frequency-domain transfer function
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{dc}}(\omega)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}I_{\mathrm{conv},k}(\omega)}
{N-\omega^2LC+j\omega CR}
\label{eq:battery_current_tf}
\end{equation}
between the summed converter-current spectrum and the resulting
dc-bus current \cite{Jin2014Control}.

In a centralized controller, $\phi_{c,k}$ in
\eqref{eq:carrier_phase} is simply an offset applied to a single
shared carrier timer, but SPB cells are physically distributed and do
not share a clock. In practice, the phase relationship established by
\eqref{eq:carrier_phase} is maintained through inter-cell
communication: a designated master cell (or
central controller) broadcasts a periodic synchronization pulse over
the serial link, and each slave cell uses this pulse to align and
offset its own local carrier timer, as demonstrated for the SPB
converter in \cite{Jin2018Distributed} and illustrated conceptually
in Fig.~\ref{fig:carrier_sync_mechanism} for a master cell and two
slave cells.

The optimum carrier displacement obtained from
\eqref{eq:carrier_phase}--\eqref{eq:harmonic_phasor_sum} is not
universal: it depends on cell count, modulation index, power factor,
winding displacement, and the specific quantity being minimized. In
particular, cancellation of one dc-link harmonic does not guarantee
minimum machine harmonic loss or minimum common-mode current. Carrier
coordination should therefore be viewed as one design variable within
a larger converter--machine modulation problem, rather than as a
self-contained optimization.

The resulting effect on the aggregate dc-link ripple current is
illustrated by simulation in
Fig.~\ref{fig:dclink_ripple_interleaving} for the same $N=3$ case.

This simulated reduction already accounts for one lumped bus/wire
inductance between the battery and the series stack, but still treats
each cell's own local interconnect to that shared bus as ideal. In a
physically distributed SPB implementation, each cell's contribution
instead reaches the shared dc path through its own additional bus-bar
and connector loop, and the parasitic inductance of that loop couples
back into the ripple-cancellation mechanism itself: the 2015 IEEE TIA study
by Wang, Li, and Han showed that in a series-connected GaN IMMD, the
interleaved structure required careful capacitor selection and tight
layout control because parasitic inductance and switching-generated
CMV limited the achievable dc-link ripple reduction
\cite{Wang2015GaNIMMD}. The same practical limitation is visible
in the earlier interleaved series-connected-drive characterization,
where parasitic loop inductance prevented full ripple cancellation and,
at switching frequencies around 100~kHz, could make the interleaved
current ripple \emph{larger} than the non-interleaved case
\cite{Wang2015ThesisDVB}. The simulated reduction of
Fig.~\ref{fig:dclink_ripple_interleaving}, which already accounts for
local capacitor filtering but not bus-bar/connector parasitic
inductance, should therefore still be read as an upper bound on what
carrier interleaving can achieve in a physically distributed
implementation, with the achievable benefit set by physical layout,
capacitor selection, and switching frequency rather than by carrier
phasing alone.

\subsection{Machine Harmonics, Voltage Utilization, and Switching Loss}

The multiphase machine identified in
Section~\ref{sec:machine_requirements} makes the harmonic problem
substantially more involved than in a conventional three-phase drive.
Nonfundamental voltage components can excite additional harmonic
subspaces with low impedance, producing copper loss and torque ripple
even when fundamental current control is fully satisfactory. Recent
integrated-modular-drive studies accordingly move toward interleaved
and discontinuous PWM techniques tailored to this multiphase
current-ripple problem
\cite{Hopkins2019DCLinkRipple,Mohamed2020DCLinkSizing,Alosa2019ModularRipple}.

At high speed, modulation must also maximize voltage utilization,
because each winding group is supplied only from its local cell
voltage $V_{\mathrm{dc},k}$, as constrained by
\eqref{eq:spb_machine_voltage_limit}. Optimized vector selection for
dual-three-phase traction machines has demonstrated the possibility of
extending the high-modulation region while simultaneously suppressing
unwanted subspace currents \cite{Chen2026HighModulationDTP}. This
objective becomes increasingly important as $N$ grows and
$V_{\mathrm{dc},k}$ decreases accordingly.

Switching loss introduces an opposing objective. DPWM and hybrid
strategies reduce the number of transitions by clamping selected
phases or by redistributing high-frequency switching among converter
modules. Related open-end-winding (OEW) work demonstrates that
coordinated modulation can reduce switching loss and unwanted
zero-sequence current simultaneously \cite{Yang2026HybridOEW,
Lee2026AlternatingDPWM}. Although the dc architecture differs from
SPB, the underlying principle of distributing switching activity among
several inverter modules is directly transferable.

\subsection{Coupling to Balancing and Common-Mode Objectives}

Modulation also interacts directly with the dc-link balancing problem
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:evolution} and formalized in
Section~\ref{sec:dclink_balancing}: differential winding-group power
must be realizable through modulation without violating local voltage
and current limits \cite{Verkroost2024PowerDistribution,
Wu2020ActiveBalancing}. Common-duty-ratio operation further shows that
propagation delay and synchronization among cells can influence the
expected voltage-sharing behavior \cite{Mocevic2024GateDelay}.
Common-mode-oriented multiphase modulation adds a further objective,
using redundant voltage vectors or coordinated zero-sequence terms to
reduce frame excitation \cite{Sun2025CMVMPC,Zheng2025MultiObjectivePWM}
--- a concern reinforced by direct measurements of electromagnetic
interference in physically integrated motor--converter assemblies
\cite{Chen2020EMCIntegrated}.

The key SPB modulation question is therefore not which conventional
PWM method performs best in isolation, but how switching decisions
should be coordinated across cells to jointly manage the objectives
above over the full operating map; a future SPB-specific
multiobjective study should report system-level converter--machine
metrics rather than a single THD or switching-loss figure of merit.
