\begin{abstract}
Increasing dc-link voltages in electric traction drives intensify semiconductor voltage stress and insulation requirements. Stacked polyphase bridge converters partition the dc-link voltage among series-connected three-phase bridges, each supplying an electrically separated machine winding group. This architecture enables lower-voltage semiconductor devices and modular converter--machine integration, but introduces coupled challenges in modulation, voltage balancing, insulation, and fault management. This paper reviews stacked polyphase bridge converters for high-voltage electric traction, bringing together literature on topology evolution, operating principles, machine integration, control, semiconductor scaling, and fault-tolerant reconfiguration. The review examines carrier-interleaving effects on dc-link ripple, conditions for switching-induced common-mode voltage cancellation, and dc-link balancing stability under centralized, common-duty-ratio, and distributed control. It also relates cell count to semiconductor voltage class and the associated converter and machine requirements. A mapping for a 1200-V dc link illustrates how increasing cell count provides access to lower-voltage device classes, while adding sensing, gate-drive, winding, and insulation requirements. The synthesis indicates that semiconductor-level gains alone are insufficient to establish a system-level advantage, and that the preferred cell count depends on the application and operating mission. Key research priorities are joint battery, converter, and machine design, together with validation against vehicle-level energy, cost, and reliability targets. These findings provide a basis for assessing where stacked polyphase bridge architectures can offer practical benefits in future traction drives.
\end{abstract}
